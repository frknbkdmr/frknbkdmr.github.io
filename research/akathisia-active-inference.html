Akathisia as active inference

Theory · in design

Akathisia is an inner restlessness that drives repetitive movement, and the movement usually fails to relieve it. That failure is the part worth explaining, and accounts pitched at the receptor do not on their own explain it. I am building an active inference account alongside them, which asks whether the disorder sits in how bodily prediction errors are turned into action rather than in the motor system itself.

The syndrome has two halves

Braude, Barnes and Gore put the clinical features through a principal components analysis in 1983, working from consecutive psychiatric admissions, and the features did not collapse onto one dimension. There is what the patient reports, an inner restlessness that sitting still does not settle. There is what you see from the other side of the desk, the weight shifting and the rocking. On the ward the gap between the two is not subtle. You can watch someone’s legs for a minute and still have to ask.

Six years later Barnes built the rating scale around that split. Objective movement gets its own items, subjective distress gets its own, and a global judgement sits on top of both. Any scale carries an assumption about what it is measuring, and this one assumes the halves are separate enough to score separately.

It also names the case where they separate completely. Pseudoakathisia is the movement with the complaint missing. Miller and Fleischhacker treat the subjective and objective components as distinct throughout their review, and their prevalence figures show what that costs: estimates run from 5 percent to nearly 37 percent, depending on which half is being counted and how strictly.

A range that wide is mostly the definition talking. Which half you count, and how strictly, moves the answer by a factor of seven. That is the part I keep coming back to. A receptor account predicts a dose of movement disorder. It does not say why the felt and the seen should be able to come apart at all.

It did not go away with the newer drugs

Demyttenaere and colleagues pooled the trials of antipsychotics licensed more recently and got a weighted incidence of 7.7 percent. That is lower than the first-generation figures and it is not close to zero.

The treatment evidence is where it gets strange. Gerolymos and colleagues ran a network meta-analysis of what actually works against antipsychotic-induced akathisia, and the drugs that beat placebo were mirtazapine (SMD −1.20), biperiden (−1.01), vitamin B6 (−0.92) and propranolol (−0.78). That is a serotonergic drug, an anticholinergic, a vitamin cofactor and a beta blocker: four targets, none of them D2. Pringsheim and colleagues land somewhere similar in the Canadian guideline, where most of the advice is about reducing or withdrawing the offending drug and then reaching for agents aimed elsewhere.

This is the point where it would be easy to overclaim, so let me be careful. None of that refutes a dopaminergic account. The drug that causes akathisia is a dopaminergic drug, and no amount of arguing about levels of description changes it. What is left is a syndrome whose two halves come apart, whose reported frequency depends on which half you count, and which responds to four drugs that do four different things. The level of description is worth arguing about.
