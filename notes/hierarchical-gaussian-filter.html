The Hierarchical Gaussian Filter, for clinical readers

Published

August 9, 2026

Modified

August 9, 2026

You are reading a computational psychiatry paper. There is a model name, a few Greek letters, and a sentence saying some parameter was higher in the patient group. This note is about what that sentence claims, and more usefully, what it does not.

No equations. The aim is not to let you build the model but to know where to look when someone else’s model produces a claim. For the mathematics and working code, Perception, Beliefs, and the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter builds it step by step with pyhgf.

The problem it solves

Suppose you watch a patient learn something. Say they are working out which of two boxes pays out more often. What you have is a sequence of trial-by-trial answers: A on this one, B on the next, then A again.

What can you read off that sequence? At the simplest you count how often they were right. That number says nothing about how the learning happened. Two patients can score identically having taken completely different routes: one revised on every new piece of evidence, the other held out and then switched all at once.

The Hierarchical Gaussian Filter assumes there is a person behind the sequence and describes how that person learns with a handful of numbers. What it models is not the behaviour but the process producing it.

What the hierarchy means

The “hierarchical” in the name refers to layers of uncertainty stacked on one another.

The bottom layer is the current state: is this box paying out right now. Above it sits the tendency: how often does this box pay out in general. The top layer is how changeable the world is, meaning how often those rules shift.

That third layer is the model’s real idea and the one with the most clinical purchase. How fast you ought to learn depends on how much the world actually changes. If the rules shift often, revising quickly is sensible. If they are fixed, the same speed has you chasing noise.

People make this adjustment constantly and the model puts a number on it. Someone who takes the world to be more volatile than it is treats a single event as too informative. Someone who takes the opposite view is slow to notice a real change.

What the parameters say clinically

The number reported most often is the learning rate, and the HGF’s difference is that the rate is not fixed. In classical learning models a person has one learning speed and it stays the same from trial to trial. Here it is recomputed on every trial, and it depends on how certain the person is at that moment and how volatile they take the world to be. Learning speeds up after a surprise and slows inside a familiar pattern.

The quantity governing this is precision. It has nothing to do with precision in the everyday sense; it measures how narrow a belief is. A narrow belief leaves little room for new evidence, a wide one leaves a lot. That is the clinically interesting part: the source of a symptom may not be the evidence but the weight given to it.

The engine is prediction error, the gap between what happened and what was expected. The raw gap is not what gets used; the precision-weighted version is. A surprise of the same size produces a different result depending on how certain the person was.

There is also response noise, which absorbs everything the model cannot explain. People do not always act on their beliefs; they get distracted, press the wrong key, waver. Having this parameter matters, because without it disorderly behaviour would look like a strange style of learning.

The appeal of these numbers is obvious. Each resembles a mechanism and attaches easily to a clinical story. The two sections below are largely against that ease.

Why model comparison is not optional

A model was fitted and numbers came out. For those numbers to mean anything you first have to know whether this model generated the data, or whether some other model would have generated it just as well.

Goodness of fit does not answer that. A flexible enough model fits any data well, and a good fit may be showing you flexibility rather than truth.

So fitting one model and reporting its numbers is not enough. Several candidate models are built, all fitted to the same data, and compared on how well each explains it. Because the comparison separately penalises complex models, adding parameters to improve the fit does not count as winning.

If a paper has no model comparison, the parameters it reports do not point at a mechanism. They are what you get when you summarise the data through that particular model. The difference is not a small one.

When to trust a finding

When a model fits, it produces a number for every parameter. That a number was produced does not mean the data carried information about that parameter. In some models two parameters move together: raise one and lower the other and the model behaves identically. Their combination can then be read off the data but neither can be read alone. The estimator still returns a number, because it has to. That number reflects setup choices more than it reflects data.

The technical name for this is identifiability. It is among the first things to ask about a computational finding.

The test is parameter recovery. You generate synthetic data from known values, run the same pipeline over it, and check whether the values you put in come back out. If they do not, the parameter cannot be measured with that design, and there is no way to discover this from real data. I ran this on my own pipeline before collecting anything, and I think the order matters: leave it until afterwards and, when the result is bad, you are holding a number you could publish, which makes it harder to drop.

Finally, parameter names. Calling a parameter “belief instability” or “learning flexibility” does not establish that it measures that. The name is the interpretation of whoever built the model. The same number can be attached to a completely different mechanism under another reading, and choosing between the two takes more than the model.

In short

The HGF’s clinical value is that it treats learning speed as an adjustment tracking how volatile a person takes the world to be, rather than as a fixed trait.

But not every number it produces is a mechanism. The estimator returns a number under any conditions; what shows the number measures something is not the number itself but the model comparison and the recovery behind it. Without both, what you are holding is a summary statistic rather than a claim about mechanism. And if the parameter’s name is confident enough, that summary statistic gets read as a mechanism.
