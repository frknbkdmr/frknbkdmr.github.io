Sensory attenuation and the force-matching task

Published

August 9, 2026

Modified

August 9, 2026

You cannot tickle yourself. There is a measurable mechanism behind that familiar observation, and where it weakens, psychiatric symptoms appear.

Tickling is only its most familiar form. The same mechanism has a laboratory measure, and that measure connects to several separate symptoms. The precision language used here is set out in the HGF note; the rig I built for the task is on the instrument page.

The observation

Touching your own palm and having someone else touch it the same way differ in nothing physical. Same pressure, same skin, same receptors. The only thing that changes is where the touch came from, and that is what drops the perceived intensity.

Blakemore and colleagues looked at this in 1998. Somatosensory cortex was more active for externally produced touch and less active for self-produced touch. In the cerebellum the pattern reversed: cerebellar activity was lower for movements that produced a touch than for movements that did not. The reading is that the cerebellum computes the sensory consequence of a movement in advance, and that computation is used to turn the incoming signal down.

The name for that turning down is sensory attenuation. Its use is this: most of the signal reaching your senses is produced by your own movements. If all of it were processed at equal weight, the externally caused signal, the one that actually carries news, would be lost inside it. Turning down the predictable is how you bring out the unpredictable.

How the task turns it into a number

The most memorable demonstration involved two people. In 2003 Shergill and colleagues had participants take turns pressing on each other’s finger, each trying to apply the force they had just felt. The force grew every turn. Nobody was exaggerating; everyone matched what they felt, but because they felt their own output as weaker than it was, each put in a little more. The paper’s title sums it up as two eyes for an eye. So the line we all used as children, that he hit first and he hit harder, was not a lie after all. It was a calibration problem.

The same effect was turned into a standard single-person task. In the force-matching task a target force is applied to the participant’s finger and they are then asked to reproduce it two ways. In the direct condition they press on their own finger with the other hand. In the slider condition a motor produces the force and they set its size with a slider.

Pressing directly, they overshoot. Through the slider they are fairly accurate. In the twenty-five-participant data published in McNaughton’s device paper the direct condition ran 0.50 N high on average and the slider condition 0.30 N low. The difference between the two conditions is the measurement.

The second condition is not decoration

Why the slider condition is needed is not obvious at first, and misreading the task usually starts here.

Error in the direct condition has more than one source. You have to remember the target force, you have to scale the force you produce, and your motor output has noise of its own. None of that is specific to prediction, and each would produce a deviation on its own. The slider condition carries the same memory and scaling load, but because the force does not come from your own hand, prediction-based attenuation is not running. Take the difference between the two and the shared effects drop out, leaving the part that belongs to prediction.

That logic was recently tested formally. Valè and colleagues built a computational model of the task on data from nearly five hundred participants, separated the individual noise sources including memory noise, and showed that the difference between conditions does isolate the predictive component. Two further things came out of the same study. Attenuated sensations vary more from trial to trial, because they carry the noise of the prediction as well. And attenuation works by division rather than subtraction: the signal is not reduced by a fixed amount, it shrinks by a factor.

What attenuation locks onto has been tested too. Bays and colleagues showed the mechanism attaches not to the movement but to the event the movement predicts. Shift the timing of the touch earlier or later and attenuation falls off. Movements that stop above the finger or pass wide of it produce no attenuation at all. The system is not saying “I am moving, turn the senses down.” It is saying “a touch is about to happen, there, now.” Kilteni and Ehrsson showed a more basic condition still: you have to count the touching hand as your own. Move the sense of ownership with the rubber hand illusion and attenuation moves with it.

The link to precision

In active inference, attenuation corresponds to lowering the precision of sensory evidence during movement. The argument that this is compulsory runs as follows: to move, your prediction that you are moving has to outweigh the sensory evidence that you are not yet moving. If that evidence is not down-weighted, the movement never starts.

Brown and colleagues reproduced the force-matching finding from this framework. When they took attenuation out of the model two things happened together: the overshoot in the task disappeared, and false inferences about agency appeared. The same setting makes movement possible and determines what counts as yours. It costs in both directions. Turn it down too little and movement does not start; too much and you cannot tell yourself from the world.

Where it sits clinically

The most instructive finding is one of the oldest. In 2000 Blakemore and colleagues compared patients with schizophrenia, patients with bipolar disorder or depression, and healthy controls. What determined the loss of attenuation was the symptom rather than the diagnosis: attenuation was absent in those with auditory hallucinations or passivity experiences and present in those without, regardless of what they were diagnosed with.

What followed widened it. Attenuation was shown to be significantly reduced in schizophrenia, and in an imaging study the loss of attenuation in secondary somatosensory cortex was predicted by the patient’s current hallucination severity. Teufel and colleagues worked in healthy people: those higher in delusional ideation were more accurate on the task, meaning they attenuated less. That last one answers two objections at once, since neither medication nor an active episode can explain it.

Leaving the picture in schizophrenia would be a mistake. In functional movement disorder attenuation is absent; patients do not experience the movement as under their own control and do not produce the overshoot that controls produce. In Parkinson’s disease attenuation is selectively impaired in the group reporting fatigue and not in the group without it, and the impairment goes with subjective fatigue and perceived effort rather than motor symptom severity. In chronic pain no group difference in magnitude was found; what separated the groups was variance.

Attenuation being disturbed across this many presentations makes it a measure tied to symptoms rather than diagnoses. It does not tell you which diagnosis a patient carries. It says something about which mechanism is disturbed. Confuse the two and the measure has promised more than it can deliver.

What the instrument contributes

Because the measurement is a difference between two conditions, instrument error common to both cancels in that difference. What does not cancel is error that depends on the direction of loading, since the two conditions do not load the sensor the same way. A ramp pressed by a motor and a ramp pressed by a person are not the same thing.

The acceptance criterion that passed by the narrowest margin on my own rig was hysteresis. That is no coincidence: hysteresis is exactly the class of error that does not cancel in the difference. The rig and its calibration record are on the instrument page.

Reading a finding

The first thing to look at in a published attenuation result is how many conditions the design had. A deviation coming out of a single-condition design cannot be called attenuation, because the second condition is what strips memory and scaling back out.

Next come the number of repetitions and the time window. McNaughton and colleagues showed in data from a hundred and thirty-eight participants that the number of repetitions can come down from eight, that the best window for extracting the matched force is 2.5 to 3.0 seconds, and that performance does not drift over the session. Where the window is not reported there is no ground for comparing two studies.

Last, whether the analysis looked only at the mean. In chronic pain the groups did not separate on magnitude but did on variance, and an analysis of means would never see a difference like that. Given that Valè and colleagues’ model says variability may be predictive signal rather than noise, this is no longer a secondary detail.
