Belief updating in psychosis and hasty decisions

Published

August 9, 2026

Modified

August 9, 2026

A delusion is described clinically as a false belief held despite the evidence. That description reports the outcome, not the mechanism. The computational question is narrower: on the path from evidence to belief, which step breaks?

The model most of this work runs on is set out in the HGF note.

The classic claim

Thirty years of literature rests largely on two jars and a handful of beads. One jar is mostly black beads, the other mostly white. The experimenter draws from one of them a bead at a time, and the participant says which jar it is once they have seen enough. The only thing measured is which bead the decision came on.

Patients with delusions decide early. In Moritz and Woodward’s sample 42 per cent of the patients with schizophrenia named the jar after a single bead; among psychiatric controls the figure was 7 per cent, among healthy controls zero. The finding is called jumping to conclusions. A meta-analysis pooling fifty-five studies points the same way: the psychosis group decides on significantly less information (g = -0.53), and the odds of extreme early deciding are four to six times higher.

How solid is the evidence

The objection is not to the group difference but to the sentence built on top of it. To say delusions arise from early deciding, early deciding has to track how severe the delusions are.

Ross and colleagues assembled individual participant data from 38 samples, 2,237 people. The relationship is significant but small: r = -0.10. The subgroups say more. It is present in the 1,754-person general population sample and absent in the 262 people with current delusions. Where the finding is weakest is precisely where it was built to apply.

An earlier meta-analysis tested four separate jumping-to-conclusions measures and found only the bead count significant. In Lincoln’s study of 71 patients and 68 controls the bead count was related to observer-rated delusion severity, but the relationship lost significance once negative symptoms or intellectual functioning entered the equation.

The schizophrenia group separates from controls. That patients with more severe delusions are hastier is a separate claim, and it has not been shown.

Is hasty the right word

Strube’s comparison had 45 patients with schizophrenia, 45 with depression and 45 healthy controls. The schizophrenia group decided on fewer beads but took longer to decide, and decision time did not correlate with bead count. Impulsivity does not explain this. What the authors point to instead is instability in how the belief forms.

Balzan’s group dug at another side of it and added an extra set of instructions to the task. The over-adjustment seen when disconfirming evidence arrives fell markedly, while early deciding did not change. Part of a finding decades old turns out to have been the instructions not being clear enough.

Speechley gathers the rest under one roof. Evidence that matches the hypothesis gains more weight than it should in a patient with delusions, while evidence that does not match keeps its ordinary weight. The same asymmetry shows up as early deciding at the start of a sequence and as over-adjustment in the middle of one.

What modelling changed

The approach of the last decade has been to stop counting beads and model the process behind the decision instead. The picture got richer and lost its order.

The largest sample is Hauke’s: 261 patients with psychotic disorders, 56 controls. Belief instability was elevated in the patients, and jumping to conclusions was related both to that and to the size of the initial uncertainty. The difference came out between patients and controls, and between those who jumped and those who did not. It did not come out between patients with few current delusions and patients with many. The most striking part of that study clinically is something else: belief instability predicted the individual response to metacognitive training.

Fromm’s design separates diagnosis from symptom deliberately, with healthy participants high and low on delusional ideation and patients high and low on delusions. Delusions were related to increased precision-weighted prediction error responses in fronto-striatal regions; the schizophrenia diagnosis was related to overestimating volatility and to a weakened neural representation of it. The two axes do not attach to the same parameter.

Sheffield looked at paranoia: prior beliefs about volatility were related to paranoia, not to unusual thought content or to anxiety. Sloan went a step further back to childhood maltreatment, and threat-type maltreatment specifically was related to expecting higher volatility. Mikus split along the symptom axis instead. Positive symptoms went with uncertainty about volatility, negative symptoms with rigid beliefs about observation noise.

The parameter is not specific to psychosis

Overestimating volatility reads like a signature of psychosis. That is a generous reading.

Gibbs-Dean’s systematic review put psychosis, anxiety and depression in one frame, and difficulty adapting to changing contingencies was present in psychosis and in anxiety alike. My own published study points the same way. Tonic volatility was elevated relative to controls in generalized anxiety disorder and correlated with state anxiety. Thirty-six participants, cross-sectional and exploratory, so it carries limited weight; the details are here.

Katthagen’s review makes it harder still. There are studies linking psychosis to too much updating and studies linking it to too little, and the variability between tasks and samples is high. If the direction of a parameter changes from study to study, that parameter cannot yet be given the name of a mechanism.

Formation or maintenance

Mashima’s group split the beads task in two. The first phase is the classic version, with beads drawn until the participant reaches a conclusion. In the second the drawing continues and the participant decides whether to withdraw the conclusion they reached; on some trials the incoming beads contradict it.

In the first phase the groups did not separate. In the second the schizophrenia group needed significantly more beads before withdrawing a conclusion, most of all in the patients whose delusions were prominent. A single study of twenty-nine patients, so caution applies. The question it asks is the right one though: the step that breaks may be abandoning a belief rather than forming one. Perhaps the operative word in the clinical definition of a delusion is not haste but irrevocability.

The measurement has not settled

For a parameter to be clinically useful, separating groups is not enough. It also has to come out similar when the same person is measured again two weeks later.

Karvelis’s group tried this. Thirty-nine healthy participants completed the same task two weeks apart and the data were modelled with the HGF. Test-retest reliability came out largely poor, no better than the behavioural measures. The main source the authors isolate is measurement noise, the kind parameter recovery reveals. Face validity holds at the group level and not at the level of the individual.

Hess’s team attributes part of the problem to the data itself. Most of these tasks produce binary responses, and binary responses carry little information. A setup that brings response times into the model as well improves the identifiability of both parameters and models substantially.

What identifiability and recovery are is explained in the HGF note. Here is what it comes to: group-level statements in this literature rest on much firmer ground than individual-level ones. The two kinds of statement change places within a paragraph rather often.

Reading a finding

I start by looking at which task was used. The beads task, the fish task, reversal learning and advice-taking tasks are all used on the assumption that they measure the same construct, and yet findings do not carry from one to another easily.

What comes next is finer. “This was elevated in schizophrenia” and “this was elevated in delusions” are no longer the same sentence; since Fromm’s design showed the two attach to separate parameters, without knowing which one was indexed you do not know what the finding says.

The least discussed question is the last one. Is the claim built at the group level or the individual level? A parameter that separates groups but cannot be repeated in the same person is a finding. It is not yet a measure.
