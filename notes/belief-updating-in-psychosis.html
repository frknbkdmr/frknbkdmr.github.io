Belief updating in psychosis and hasty decisions

Published

August 9, 2026

In the clinic a delusion is described as a false belief held despite the evidence, but that description stops at the outcome and says nothing about the mechanism. The computational question is narrower: on the road from evidence to belief, which step goes wrong?

This is my thesis topic, so I am writing as someone who both reads this literature and is trying to get into it, and I have tried to keep apart the places where the ground is solid from the places where it is not.

The classic claim

Most of thirty years of literature rests on two jars and a handful of beads. One jar is mostly black, the other mostly white. The experimenter draws one bead at a time, and when the participant feels they have seen enough they say which jar it is. The only thing measured is which bead the decision came at.

Deluded patients decide early. In Moritz and Woodward’s sample, 42 per cent of patients with schizophrenia named the jar after a single bead; in psychiatric controls the figure was 7 per cent and in healthy participants zero. The finding is called jumping to conclusions. A meta-analysis pooling fifty-five studies points the same way: the psychosis group decides on significantly less information (g = −0.53), and the odds of deciding extremely early run four to six times higher.

How solid is the evidence

The objection here is not to the group difference but to the sentence built on top of it. To say “delusions come out of early decisions” you need early decisions to track the severity of the delusion.

Ross and colleagues gathered individual participant data from 38 samples, 2237 people. The relationship is significant but small, r = −0.10. The subgroups say more: it shows up in the 1754-person general population sample but not in the 262 people with current delusions, so the finding is weakest exactly where it was built to apply.

An older meta-analysis tested four separate jumping-to-conclusions measures and found only the bead count significant. In Lincoln’s study of 71 patients and 68 controls the bead count was related to observer-rated delusion severity, but the relationship lost significance once negative symptoms or IQ entered the equation.

So there is no problem with the schizophrenia group separating from controls; that patients with more severe delusions are hastier is a different claim altogether, and we cannot say it has been shown.

What early deciding is a sign of

Strube’s comparison had 45 patients with schizophrenia, 45 with depression and 45 healthy controls. The schizophrenia group decided on fewer beads but took longer to decide, and there was no relationship between decision time and bead count either. Impulsivity does not explain this; the reason the authors point to is instability in how the belief forms. Reasoning does not explain much either. When Kemp and colleagues compared deluded patients with controls on three separate logic tests, both groups made plenty of errors and the gap, in the authors’ own words, was surprisingly small; the deluded group endorsed more fallacies only when the content was emotive, and they fell for the conjunction fallacy less often than controls did. Sixteen patients and sixteen controls, so a small study.

Balzan’s group dug at another side of it and added an extra set of instructions to the task. The over-adjustment seen when disconfirming evidence arrives dropped markedly while early deciding did not change, which means part of a finding that stood for years was the instructions not being written clearly enough.

Speechley gathers the rest under one roof. Evidence that fits the hypothesis gains more weight than it should in a deluded patient, while evidence that does not fit stays at normal weight. The same asymmetry shows up as early deciding at the start of the sequence and as over-adjustment in the middle of it.

What modelling changed

The approach of the last decade has been to stop counting beads and model the process behind the decision, which enriched the picture and disordered it.

The largest sample is Hauke’s, 261 patients with psychosis and 56 controls. Belief instability was high in patients, and jumping to conclusions was related both to that and to the size of the initial uncertainty. The difference came out between patients and controls, and between those who jumped and those who did not; it did not come out between patients with few current delusions and patients with many. The most interesting part of the same study clinically was that belief instability predicted the response to metacognitive training at the individual level.

Fromm’s design deliberately separates diagnosis from symptom: healthy people with few and with many delusional ideas, patients with few and with many. Delusions were related to an increase in the precision-weighted prediction error response in fronto-striatal regions; a schizophrenia diagnosis was related to volatility being estimated higher than it is and to its neural representation being weakened, so the two axes do not attach to the same parameter.

When Sheffield looked at paranoia, prior beliefs about volatility were related to paranoia and not to unusual thought content or to anxiety. Sloan went a step further back to childhood adversity and showed that adversity of the threat type in particular was related to expecting higher volatility. Mikus drew the line along the symptom axis instead; positive symptoms were related to uncertainty about volatility, negative symptoms to rigid beliefs about observation noise.

The same parameter turns up elsewhere

Estimating volatility high is read now and then as the signature of psychosis, but that is a generous reading.

Gibbs-Dean’s systematic review put psychosis, anxiety and depression in the same frame; difficulty adapting to changing probabilities was there in psychosis and in anxiety alike. As a doctor who sees anxious patients in the clinic I did not find that surprising. My own published study pointed the same way: in generalised anxiety disorder tonic volatility was higher than in controls and was related to state anxiety. Thirty-six people, cross-sectional and exploratory, so it does not carry much weight; the details are here.

Katthagen’s review makes it harder still. There are studies relating psychosis to too much updating and studies relating it to too little, and the variability between tasks and samples is high; a parameter whose direction changes from study to study is not one we can yet give the name of a mechanism.

Formation or maintenance

Mashima’s group split the beads task in two. The first stage is the classic version, with beads drawn until the participant reaches a conclusion. In the second, drawing continues and the participant decides whether to abandon the conclusion they reached; on some trials the beads arriving contradict it.

The groups did not separate in the first stage. In the second, the schizophrenia group wanted significantly more beads before abandoning the conclusion they had reached, most of all in patients whose delusions were prominent. Twenty-nine patients in a single study, so caution is in order, but the question it asks holds up: the step that goes wrong may not be forming the belief but giving it up, in which case the word to underline in the clinical definition of a delusion would be irreversibility rather than haste. I write that as a possibility waiting to be tested rather than as a claim.

How reliable is the measurement

For a parameter to be of use in the clinic it is not enough that it separates groups; it also has to come out similar when the same person is measured again two weeks later.

Karvelis’s group tried this. Thirty-nine healthy participants completed the same task twice, two weeks apart, and the data were modelled with the HGF. Test-retest reliability came out largely low and was no better than the behavioural measures; the main source the authors single out is the measurement noise that parameter recovery reveals. So a measure with face validity at the group level does not carry the same thing at the individual level.

Hess’s team put part of the problem down to the data itself. Most of these tasks produce a binary response, and a binary response carries little information. A setup that also brings response times into the model markedly improves how well parameters and models can be told apart.

What identifiability and recovery are is set out in the HGF note. What it comes to here is that group-level statements in this literature stand on much firmer ground than individual-level ones, and that the two kinds of statement change places in the same paragraph often enough.

Reading a finding

I start by looking at the name of the task. The beads task, the fish task, reversal learning and advice-taking tasks are used on the assumption that they measure the same concept, when findings do not carry across from one to another easily.

What comes next is finer. “This was high in schizophrenia” and “this was high in delusions” are no longer the same sentence; given that Fromm’s design showed the two attach to separate parameters, without knowing which is being indexed you do not know what the finding says.

The least discussed question is the last, whether the claim is built at the group level or the individual level. Two schools can differ in average height without that telling you which school the child in front of you attends; in the same way, a parameter that separates groups but cannot be repeated in the same person counts as a finding and not as a measure.

I will update this page as the thesis moves along. I expect some of what I have written above to be contradicted by my own data, frankly.
